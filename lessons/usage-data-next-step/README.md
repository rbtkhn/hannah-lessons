## Tutor-only reference: definitions and evidence limits

Definitions:

- **Assigned accounts / 已分配账号:** people assigned an account during the reporting week.
- **Weekly active users / 每周活跃用户:** assigned people who opened the tool at least once that week, counted once each. This is the fictional dataset’s definition, not a universal product definition.
- **Activity rate / 活跃率:** weekly active users divided by assigned accounts for the same tool and week.
- **Percentage points / 百分点:** the difference between two percentages. Keep it distinct from relative percentage change.

Additional evidence: an optional introduction session for TaskBoard took place between the weeks. Ten accounts were added by week 2. Attendance, individual usage, task outcomes, time saved and reasons for opening the tools are unknown. The table is aggregate: it does not show whether the same individuals were active both weeks. No personal records are available or required.

## One meaningful invitation

The opening already asks this question. Do not repeat it after an answer; use it only if a later return needs orientation. Ask one question at a time: “从这些材料里，你会先向同事说明哪一个变化？” / “What change would you explain to a colleague first?”

After responding to their observation, offer one useful continuation: explore an uncertainty, choose a next question, or finish. Avoid piling three tasks into a single turn. A full brief is optional.

## Worked guidance — reveal when useful or requested

TaskBoard:

- Active users: 10 → 15, an increase of 5, or 50% relative to 10.
- Assigned accounts: 20 → 30, an increase of 10, also 50%.
- Activity rate: 10/20 = 50%; 15/30 = 50%. Change: **0 percentage points**.

BriefDesk:

- Active users: 6 → 7, an increase of 1.
- Activity rate: 6/10 = 60%; 7/10 = 70%. Change: **10 percentage points**.
- The relative increase in its active-user count is 1/6, about 16.7%; this is different from a ten-percentage-point rate change. This extra distinction is optional, not a required exercise.

Supported interpretation: TaskBoard reached more active people while the proportion of assigned accounts active remained the same. BriefDesk’s activity rate rose in a small group. Neither pattern proves usefulness, productivity, sustained adoption or that the introduction session caused a change. These two weeks alone do not establish a durable trend. A higher activity rate alone is not a reason to declare one tool better.

Do not turn the supplied limitations into a demand for a particular answer. Accept a different defensible question or recommendation and examine what evidence would support it.

## Optional next step or brief

Invite: “如果只能再了解一件事，你会问什么？也可以先听我的建议。” / “If we could find out one more thing, what would you ask? You can hear my suggestion first.”

Possible tutor examples, supplied only when helpful:

- Ask what tasks people used the tool for and whether it helped complete those tasks.
- Clarify which accounts were added and whether the usage definitions are consistent.
- Ask about introduction-session attendance, while explaining that attendance alone would not prove causation.

These are proposed questions in a fictional exercise, not instructions to contact anyone or retrieve real user-level data. No permission to investigate a workplace is implied.

If wanted, help produce three short sentences: **observation, uncertainty, next step**. Example: “TaskBoard’s active count rose from 10 to 15, while its activity rate stayed at 50%. We do not know whether the introduction session helped or whether users benefited. I would first ask what work the tool supported and what evidence of usefulness is available.”

Label this as a tutor-written example. If the learner selects, revises or challenges it, describe their actual contribution rather than claiming they independently wrote or calculated the whole brief.
